# T3-03 — The Flour Sack (Reskinned: The Weary Soot Sprite)

**Tool:** Krita
**Goal:** The classic bridge exercise between the bouncing ball and the walk cycle — used by Disney's training program for generations and still the standard second/third assignment in every animation school. A flour sack is the simplest possible *character*: it has weight, attitude, and a squashy body, but no limbs, face, or anatomy to draw wrong. It's where you learn that the ball principles + a pose = *acting*.

## The reskin

A flour sack is already 90% of a **soot sprite** or a small **stuffed familiar** — pick whichever reads Ghibli to you. A plump rounded rectangle with two floppy corner "ears" (the sack's corners) is the classic construction; keep it that simple. If you use a soot sprite, give it the sack's proportions (squat, bottom-heavy) rather than a perfect circle — the corners/tufts are what make attitude readable.

## Why this exercise exists (what it adds over the ball)

The bouncing ball taught **physics** — timing, spacing, squash/stretch on a symmetric object. The sack adds:
- **Weight distribution**: the sack settles, slumps, and leans. Its mass *pools* toward the bottom when at rest.
- **Attitude/pose**: a sack can look tired, alert, defeated, or eager purely through posture — this is your first *acting* exercise, and acting-through-posture is the entire soul of Ghibli's background creatures (the soot sprites in *Spirited Away* emote with nothing but posture and speed).
- **Asymmetric squash & stretch**: unlike the ball, a squashing sack keeps a top and a bottom — squash bunches it downward and outward, stretch pulls the corners apart.

## The exercise (two parts)

### Part A — pose test (still drawings first)
Draw your sack/sprite in 4 distinct attitudes: **proud/alert**, **exhausted/slumped**, **curious (leaning to peek at something)**, **frightened (recoiling)**. Rules: no face, no limbs — posture only. Line of action first (T2-04), silhouette test each one. This is fast — the point is proving to yourself that posture alone carries emotion.

### Part B — the jump
Animate the sack doing one jump: settle → anticipate (crouch/compress) → launch (stretch) → airborne arc → land (big squash) → recover into a settled pose. Roughly 20-30 frames at 12fps.

1. Key poses first: settled, deepest crouch, launch stretch, arc apex, impact squash, final settle. Six drawings.
2. Check the keys ALONE in playback (each held several frames) — the action should already read from just these. If it doesn't, fix the keys before in-betweening; no amount of in-betweens rescues weak keys. This keys-first discipline is the workflow from T1-02 becoming real.
3. In-between it, spacing tight near the crouch/settle (slow) and wide during launch/fall (fast) — identical logic to the ball.
4. **The anticipation matters most**: the crouch before the jump is what sells weight and intent. Try a version with a 2-frame crouch and one holding the crouch 6+ frames — feel how the longer anticipation makes the jump feel *decided* rather than mechanical. (Anticipation is one of the 12 principles — this exercise is where it clicks.)
5. Optional flourish: the corner "ears" drag a frame or two behind the body's motion — trailing on the way up, flopping down on landing. That's **follow-through/overlapping action**, another of the 12, and it's charming even done crudely.

## Common mistakes

- Sack stays rigid in the air (it should stretch on launch, relax at apex, elongate slightly falling).
- Landing recovery too fast — weight needs a couple of frames of settle/wobble before stillness, or it reads weightless.
- Poses in Part A that only read from the front — test the silhouette.

## Check-in

Render Part B and share it with the Part A poses. The two review questions that matter: does the jump feel *heavy*, and does the anticipation read as intent? Expect a revision pass on the landing — everyone's first landing is too fast.
