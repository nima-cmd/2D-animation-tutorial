# T2-04 — Drawing *for Animation*: Gesture, Line of Action, Silhouette, Model Sheets

**Tool:** Paper + Krita
**Goal:** The skills that separate "drawing" from "drawing for animation." A beautiful drawing you can only produce once, slowly, is useless to an animator — animation drawing optimizes for *speed, consistency, and readable motion* over polish. This is also where the classic animation-drawing texts come in (Preston Blair's *Animation* book, the gesture tradition — see [references](../../references.md)).

## Concept 1 — Gesture and the Line of Action

Every strong animation pose is built on a single curved line — the **line of action** — that runs through the whole body and states the pose's energy in one stroke. C-curves and S-curves feel alive; straight vertical lines feel stiff. This is rule #1 in Preston Blair's book, and you can see it instantly in Ghibli action frames (a running Chihiro is one big forward-leaning C).

**Drill — 30-second gestures:** pull up screenshots/paused frames of Frieren or any Ghibli film with characters mid-motion. For each, take 30 seconds *maximum*: draw the line of action first, then wrap the loosest possible figure around it. Do 15-20 per session. The time limit is the teacher — it forces energy over accuracy, which is exactly the reflex animation needs. (This is standard gesture-drawing practice; Proko's free gesture videos teach the same method with live models if you want a deeper treatment.)

## Concept 2 — Silhouette

A pose reads or it doesn't, and it's decided by the silhouette. Classic test: fill your drawing with solid black — can a stranger still tell what the character is doing? Animators design poses to read in silhouette because in motion, viewers get a fraction of a second per drawing.

**Drill:** take 5 of your gesture drawings, fill each solid black in Krita (or scribble over on paper). For any that turn into unreadable blobs, redraw the pose *opening it up* — move limbs off the body, turn the pose sideways, exaggerate. Note that this is why lesson T2-03's character design brief says "easy silhouette to read."

## Concept 3 — Drawing it AGAIN (consistency, the animator's real skill)

An illustrator draws a character once. An animator draws it 200 times and every one must look like the same character. The tools for this are boring and mechanical, which is good news — they're learnable:

- **Construction discipline** (from T2-03): always build from the same simple forms in the same order. Consistency comes from the *scaffold* being identical, not from a good eye.
- **The model sheet**: a reference drawing of your character from multiple angles with key measurements marked (how many heads tall, where the eyeline sits, how big the hands are relative to the face). Studios enforce these ruthlessly; Frieren's production would have model sheets for every named character. You'll make a mini one below.
- **Checking against ghosts**: onion skinning (T1-02) isn't just for motion — it's how you keep frame 2's head the same size as frame 1's.

**Drill — the repetition test:** draw your project character (designed in T2-03) five times in a row, same pose, ~2 minutes each, WITHOUT tracing — construction only. Line them up. The drift you see between copies is your consistency gap; it shrinks with exactly this drill and nothing else.

## Concept 4 — The mini model sheet (the deliverable)

Make a one-page model sheet for your project character, in Krita:
1. Front view and 3/4 view (you have these from T2-03 — redraw or refine them).
2. A side/profile view (new — and harder than it looks; keep the construction scaffold visible on a layer underneath).
3. Horizontal guide lines running across all three views: top of head, eyeline, chin, shoulders, hips, ground. All three views must agree with the lines.
4. One corner box: the character's head at a smaller size, plus their hand next to their face for scale.
5. Keep the design SIMPLE — every detail you add here is a detail you redraw hundreds of times in Track 3. Frieren's design is a good complexity ceiling: distinctive silhouette (the twin tails, the robe), almost no interior detail.

This sheet becomes the reference you keep open on-screen through every Track 3 lesson involving the character.

## What "done" looks like

- Gestures: energy visible in 30 seconds, line of action first, every time.
- At least one pose that passes the silhouette test cleanly.
- Five construction-built repetitions of your character that a stranger would call "the same character."
- The mini model sheet, exported as a PNG you can keep open while animating.

## Check-in

Share the model sheet and the five-in-a-row repetition test. Those two artifacts tell an instructor everything about readiness for character animation — and the model sheet gets a real review pass, since flaws in it get inherited by every animation drawing after.

This completes Track 2's core sequence — the drills (gestures especially) stay in rotation as ongoing warm-ups, but the track gates nothing further. Track 3's walk cycle is where all of this gets used in anger.
