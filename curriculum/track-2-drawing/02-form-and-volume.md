# T2-02 — Form & Volume: Why Drawings Look Flat and Dead (and the Fix)

**Tool:** Paper first, then Krita
**Goal:** Attack the exact problem you named — drawings that come out "flat and dead" — at its actual root. This is the single highest-leverage drawing lesson in the whole curriculum.

## The diagnosis

Flat, dead drawings are almost never a talent problem. They're nearly always the same three specific, fixable habits:

1. **Drawing outlines instead of forms.** A flat drawing is a *symbol* of a thing (the idea "arm" as a tube-shaped outline); a live drawing is a *form in space* (a cylinder you could walk around). Your hand draws what your brain models — if the brain models a 2D shape, no amount of rendering rescues it.
2. **No overlap.** In real space, nearer things overlap farther things — an arm crossing the torso, a chin overlapping the neck, one leg in front of the other. Beginners unconsciously avoid overlap (drawing figures with everything splayed out and visible), which reads as paper-doll flat. Overlap is the *cheapest* depth cue that exists — it costs zero rendering skill.
3. **Uniform, dead line.** One even line weight everywhere is a wireframe. Varying it — heavier on the underside/shadow side and where forms press together, lighter on top edges and far edges — makes the same drawing pop off the page with no shading at all. Look at any Frieren key visual: the linework is *thin overall but not uniform*, and that's a deliberate choice, not an accident.

Ghibli and Frieren are actually ideal style targets for this lesson, because neither relies on heavy rendering — their depth comes almost entirely from **construction, overlap, and line**, plus color/light handled by painters. Which means the fix for "flat and dead" in this style is exactly the three habits above, not learning to render like a portrait artist.

## Drills

Do each on paper first, then repeat digitally (this doubles as Track 4 pen mileage).

1. **Contour-wrapped forms**: draw a cylinder, then draw 4-5 curved cross-contour lines wrapping around it like rings on a barrel. Repeat for a sphere (think latitude/longitude) and a box. Then draw a bent cylinder (an arm) with wrapping contours following the bend. This drill *forces* the brain into 3D-form mode — it's uncomfortable at first, which is the point. (Drawabox's texture/form intersection work drills the same muscle.)
2. **Overlap stacks**: draw a row of 5-6 simple hills or rounded stones where each one partially overlaps the one behind it. Notice how depth appears with zero shading. Then do it with soot sprites — a little crowd of them, front ones overlapping back ones, back ones drawn *slightly smaller and slightly higher on the page*. You've just used overlap + size + placement: three depth cues, no rendering.
3. **The T-junction rule**: wherever one form passes behind another, the far form's line *stops* at the near form's edge (making a T shape). Go back to your overlap stack and check every intersection is a clean T — no lines "kissing" ambiguously or passing through.
4. **Line weight pass**: take three drawings from drill 1-2 and go over them with a heavier line on: the underside of each form, the outer silhouette, and anywhere two forms touch. Keep interior/top/far lines light. Compare before and after — this is the biggest visible payoff-per-minute in this whole track.
5. **One simple form under light**: a single sphere on a ground plane, one light source, exactly three values (light side / shadow side / cast shadow on the ground). No blending, no gradients — three flat regions, like a cel. This is genuinely all the shading this curriculum needs: Ghibli-style shading IS mostly two-to-three flat tones with a hard edge (look at any character in sunlight in *Totoro* — face is two values).

## Digital pass notes

In Krita, do the line-weight drill with a pressure-sensitive brush and let pen pressure make the weight variation instead of re-stroking — this is where the tablet starts genuinely beating paper, and it's a Track 4 checkpoint in disguise: if pressure feels uncontrollable, that's a pressure-curve setting issue (see T4-01, step 3.4), not a hand problem.

## What "done" looks like

Take any old drawing of yours that felt flat, and redraw it applying: wrapped forms, at least one deliberate overlap, T-junctions, weighted lines. It doesn't need to be *good* — it needs to be visibly less flat than the original, and you should be able to say *which habit* made the difference.

## Check-in

Share the before/after pair. That comparison is the ideal thing for an instructor to look at — it shows exactly which of the three habits has landed and which needs another pass.
