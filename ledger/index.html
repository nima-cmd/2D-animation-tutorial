Front matter

On the four tracks, and why they are not in order

Question asked: “Track 4 is done and we’re on Track 3, but we never did Track 2 — are we going out of order, or am I reading these wrong?”

The numbers are subject labels, not a sequence. Only Track 3 is a spine; the others are threads pulled in at the moment they are needed.

  • Track 4 (pen & tablet) came first because it gates everything — no usable pen, no drawing of any kind. It is a parallel thread, not a phase.
  • Track 3 starts early on purpose. The classic exercises — bouncing ball, flour sack — are deliberately built from circles and blobs. The tradition begins there precisely so that motion can be taught with shapes that demand no drawing skill. Waiting until you can draw well before animating anything is backwards.
  • Track 2 (drawing) is deferred, not skipped. It unlocks when an animation exercise starts hurting without it: T3-03 the flour sack needs T2-02 form & volume (a sack must feel like it has mass), and T3-04 the walk cycle needs T2-03 construction (you cannot animate a body you cannot build). The bouncing prop needs neither — it is a circle.

The principle underneath: learn a skill at the moment you have a concrete use for it, rather than doing four lessons on faith. A lesson marked locked in the Progress ledger therefore means “not needed yet,” not “not earned yet.”

Chapter I

Track 1 — Tools & workflow

The universal machinery of digital 2D animation — the tools found in every serious program (Krita, OpenToonz, Toon Boom…), so that graduating to OpenToonz means relearning button locations, not ideas.

Onion skinning

The digital lightbox. It shows faint coloured “ghosts” of the neighbouring frames beneath the one you are drawing, so each new drawing can be lined up against the last — the single tool that makes smooth frame-by-frame motion possible.

  • Red ghost = the previous frame; green ghost = the next (set by the colour keys at the bottom of the Onion Skins docker). Standing on a later frame, the earlier drawing sits behind you in red.
  • Two switches must both be on or nothing shows: the Onion Skins docker’s main toggle, and the per-layer light-bulb in the Layers docker.
  • Gotcha we hit: that light-bulb is camouflaged — far-left edge of the layer row, to the left of the eyeball, and it only appears on hover. If the ghosts won’t show, this bulb is almost always why.
  • Choose which neighbours appear with the number strip (-2 -1 0 1 2) in the docker — the negative numbers are the previous frames.

Holds, and animating “on twos”

A drawing lasts until the next keyframe replaces it — the gap between keyframes is the exposure. Two distinct uses of that gap:

  • A hold is the same drawing kept on screen while nothing changes — deliberate stillness (a character breathing, a landscape settling).
  • “On twos” is the working cadence for movement: a new drawing every 2 frames, each in a new position. The subject is moving the whole time — you’re just making ~12 drawings a second instead of 24. Not the same as a hold.

Question asked: “If it’s moving, why hold every drawing for two frames — why twos at all?”

  • The eye can’t tell. ~12 image-changes/second already reads as smooth; doubling to 24 adds little for most motion.
  • Half the drawings. Hand-drawn means one picture at a time — twos is half the labour, which is why every studio (Disney, Ghibli, anime) standardised on it.
  • The look. On-twos crispness is part of the hand-drawn visual language; on-ones can feel uncannily “video-like.”
  • When to break it: fast motion → drop to ones (else it strobes); calm → longer holds. Twos is the baseline, not a rule. A Frieren scene mixes all three — dialogue on twos, a slash on ones, a quiet gaze on a big hold.
  • Spacing = speed: the distance a subject jumps between successive drawings is what sells how fast it’s going. Spacing so wide it strobes is your cue to switch to ones.

Reusing drawings — duplicate frames & layers-as-cels

Question asked: “Drawing the body by hand took ages — do I have to draw the whole thing over again on the next frame?” No. Two ways animation avoids redrawing what doesn’t change:

  • Create Duplicate Frame copies the entire current drawing into the new frame — you then erase/redraw only the part that changes (for a blink, just the eyes). The body comes along for free; you never redraw it.
  • Layers as cels (the pro method): put the parts that don’t move on their own layer (the fuzzy body) and hold it — one drawing, never redrawn — and animate only the moving part (the eyes) on a layer above. This is exactly how hand-drawn studios worked: stacked transparent cels over a single held background painting. For slow, laborious drawing this is the biggest time-saver there is — draw the hard part once.

Re-timing: moving keyframes without redrawing anything

Timing is editable after the fact. A drawing is not welded to its frame — drag a keyframe along the timeline and the drawing travels with it. An entire re-time costs zero new drawings, which makes timing the cheapest thing in animation to experiment with, and therefore the thing to experiment with most.

  • Work in the direction you are moving. Shifting keyframes earlier (0,3,6,9,12 → 0,2,3,5,8), go left to right: each move vacates the slot the next one needs, so nothing ever lands on an occupied frame. Shifting later, go right to left. Getting this backwards makes one keyframe drop onto another and destroy it.
  • Save a copy before re-timing (-retimed suffix). Keeping the original beside the new version is what lets you play the two back to back — and the comparison is where the learning actually is.
  • If you save over the original anyway (done here, 2026-08-11): Krita keeps a backup beside the file named <name>.kra~, which holds the document as it was before the most recent save. The original was recovered from it intact. But the window is exactly one save wide — the next save rewrites .kra~ with the new version and the original is gone for good. On discovering the mistake, copy the backup somewhere safe before touching Krita again, and immediately File → Save As onto the intended name so the open document stops pointing at the file you meant to protect.
  • A .kra is a zip. The exact keyframe times sit in <name>/layers/<layer>.keyframes.xml. For a pure re-time — where no drawing changed — those times are the whole edit, so the timing can be verified to the frame without rendering anything.

In-betweens (met early, in the blink)

Between two key drawings you can add in-between drawings that ease from one to the next — a blink reads far smoother as open → half → shut → half → open than as a hard open/shut cut. How those in-betweens are spaced in time (even vs. uneven, fast vs. slow) is what makes motion feel alive or mechanical — first met here in T1-02, drilled properly in Track 3’s principles lessons.

Chapter III

Track 3 — Animation fundamentals

The classic milestone sequence from the Disney tradition — Thomas & Johnston’s twelve principles, Williams’ Survival Kit — worked here as ball, sack, walk, scene.

Frame rate is the grid; cadence is what you do on it

Question asked: “Was I supposed to do this at 12fps? I don’t understand where fps determines whether I draw in twos or threes, or how I’d choose my fps.”

These are two different numbers, and untangling them is most of what timing is:

  • Frame rate is the grid — how finely the second is divided. At 24fps there are 24 slots per second, each ≈42ms. It is a property of the document, set once.
  • Ones / twos / threes is the cadence — how many of those slots each drawing occupies. It is not a setting anywhere. It is purely where you place your keyframes. That is the entire mechanism.
  • 24fps, a drawing every 1 slot → 24 drawings/sec — on ones
  • 24fps, a drawing every 2 slots → 12 drawings/sec — on twos (the standard)
  • 24fps, a drawing every 3 slots → 8 drawings/sec — on threes

So “12fps” as a working rate means 12 drawings per second, achieved by spacing keyframes 2 apart on a 24fps timeline — not by setting the document to 12. Setting the document to 12 would make 12 drawings/sec a ceiling: a fast action needing ones becomes impossible. Keeping the fine grid lets you choose cadence per shot — dialogue on twos, a slash on ones, a held gaze on a long hold.

The rule: fps is the grid, cadence is what you do on it. Set the grid once (24 — film and anime standard), then vary cadence constantly. Choose 30 or 60 only for video and game work, never for traditional 2D.

Where to read it in Krita: Animation Timeline docker, bottom bar, right-hand end — a small box reading 24 (tooltip Frame rate). The percentage box beside it is play speed, a preview-only slow-motion that changes nothing in the file — do not confuse them. Definitive fallback: a .kra is a zip; inside maindoc.xml sits <framerate value="24">.

Changing cadence within a single action

Cadence is not one choice per shot — it can shift inside one movement, and that shift is what makes motion feel alive. The re-timed blink:

  • open @0 → half @2 — twos
  • half @2 → shut @3 — ones (the lids slamming shut: the fastest instant)
  • shut @3 → half @5 — twos
  • half @5 → open @8 — threes (the eye lazily reopening)

Contrast the first attempt, which used threes for all four gaps. The eye travelled at the same rate for the whole blink — and real things do not. Uniform cadence is the signature of mechanical motion. Fast in, slow out, is the signature of a living one.

Note: those numbers were later corrected against measured physiology to 0 / 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 — see Deriving frame counts from measurement below. The principle is unchanged; the arithmetic was off.

Deriving frame counts from measurement — the five steps

Question asked: “Why those keyframes? I don’t want to be told how to make something look right — I want the toolset to control it myself.” The correct answer to “why these numbers” is never taste; it is an arithmetic chain that can be re-run on any action:

  1. What does the real thing take, in milliseconds? Measure it, film yourself, or find a study.
  2. Divide by the frame duration — at 24fps one frame is 41.67 ms — to get a frame count.
  3. Find the asymmetry. Nearly every action has a fast phase and a slow phase. Split the frame budget by the measured ratio, not by halves.
  4. Within each phase, is it accelerating or decelerating? Accelerating → the time gaps shrink. Decelerating → the gaps grow.
  5. Then stylise. Usually compress for snap. This is the only step where taste enters — steps 1–4 can be objectively wrong; step 5 cannot.

The blink, derived properly (and an instructor error corrected)

Claude first asserted from memory that a blink closes twice as fast as it reopens (1:2). Checked against measurement, that was wrong. VanderWerf et al. (2003), using a magnetic search coil on the lid with EMG of the orbicularis oculi, measured the spontaneous blink as:

  • total 334 ± 67 ms
  • closing (down) phase 92 ± 17 ms
  • opening (up) phase 242 ± 55 ms — nearly three times the close, a ratio of about 1 : 2.6

Re-running the five steps at 24fps: total 334 ÷ 41.67 = 8 frames; close 92 ÷ 41.67 = 2 frames; open 242 ÷ 41.67 = 6 frames. Distributing the reopen with widening gaps (2, then 4) gives:

open @0 · half @1 · shut @2 · half @4 · open @8 — 333 ms against a measured 334 ms.

The first attempt (0/2/3/5/8) closed one frame too slowly and reopened one frame too briefly. The lesson is not the numbers but the method: a claim from memory produced plausible keyframes that were quietly wrong, and only the measurement exposed it.

A limit worth knowing: at 24fps the entire closing phase is 2 frames — the grid barely resolves it, leaving no room to show the lids accelerating. This is precisely why animators frequently cut straight from open to shut with no in-between: the physiology was never available at this resolution to begin with.

speed = spacing ÷ timing — the two levers

Observed by the user, unprompted, on comparing the three versions of the blink: “keyframes closer together for faster animation — the first three keyframes, the eyes closing, are back to back, and that’s where we want our speed. Inversely, when the eyes open we want to go slower, and the keyframes are spaced farther apart.” Correct — and worth stating in its general form, because the blink is the special case:

  • Spacing — how far the subject moved in position between two drawings.
  • Timing — how many frames sit between them.
  • Speed = spacing ÷ timing. Distance over time; literally velocity. Which means there are two independent levers on speed, and most exercises only hand you one.

Why the blink only offered one: its five drawings are fixed, so the positional distance between “open” and “half-shut” is baked into the artwork and cannot change without redrawing. Spacing was frozen; time was the only variable. Compressing the frames covers the same distance in less time — faster.

The bouncing prop (T3-02) inverts this exactly: timing is held constant (on twos throughout) and the ball is drawn in a different place each time — gaps wide while it falls fast, bunching tight as it slows at the top of the arc. Same drawings per second, and it still reads as accelerating and decelerating. That is spacing working alone, with timing frozen.

Blink = the time lever. Ball = the position lever. Animation is driving both at once.

Note also the shape of the reopening: gaps of 2 then 4 — doubling. Widening gaps are a deceleration curve, and drawing them out as a diagram is precisely Williams’ spacing chart.

Inverting the asymmetry — where an emotion actually lives

Found by the user while deriving a drowsy blink from the same five drawings, reasoning about what a tired eyelid is doing rather than reaching for “the same thing, slower”: “you start fighting to keep your eyes open, so the lids drop slowly; then you lose the battle and it accelerates shut; you hold there as you doze; then the last two frames are in succession to mirror the frantic, jolting reopening as you catch yourself.”

  • A waking blink: fast close (92 ms), slow open (242 ms) — ratio ≈ 1 : 2.6.
  • A drowsy blink: slow close (208 ms), fast open (83 ms) — ratio ≈ 2.5 : 1.

The emotion is not in the duration; it is in the reversal. An alert eye snaps shut and drifts open. An eye fighting sleep sags shut and snaps open. Stretching every gap uniformly would only have produced a slow blink — the characterisation comes from flipping which phase is the fast one. Look for the asymmetry first, and ask whether the feeling inverts it.

And note what carried the meaning: a hold. The most expressive moment in the shot is the one where nothing moves. That is ma — Miyazaki’s pause — arrived at from the opposite direction, and a reminder that stillness is an active choice rather than an absence of animation. Corollary: a jolt reads as frantic only in proportion to the stillness it interrupts, so lengthening the hold buys a sharper recovery for free.

Timing is read relatively, not absolutely

Found by the user on lengthening the drowsy blink’s hold: “when I increased the length of the shut, I needed to further slow down the shutting of the eyes at the beginning, and add one extra hold before the eyes open, to keep things in scale and balance.”

The close had not been touched — yet it now felt too fast. A duration has no speed of its own; it is perceived against its neighbours. 208 ms beside a 208 ms hold reads as measured; the identical 208 ms beside an 875 ms hold reads as hurried.

Consequence: stretching one element silently changes the read of every element around it. Timing must be rebalanced as a whole, never adjusted one gap at a time. This is why a shot is judged on playback rather than in the numbers — and it is the same reason a jolt reads as frantic only in proportion to the stillness it interrupts.

Re-using drawings out of order (non-monotonic keyframes)

A keyframe need not introduce a new drawing — it can re-place one already made. A sequence may therefore double back: open → half → open → half → shut.

Why it matters: a lid that only ever descends is a surrender; a lid that briefly climbs back is a struggle. Any action containing conflict, hesitation, tremor, or repetition needs this — and it costs no new artwork, only copied keyframes. It is the technique on which a nervous read almost entirely depends, since nervousness is irregularity and irregularity requires revisiting positions.

Timing as expression — is there a right and a wrong?

Question asked: “Is timing what gives a film its signature? Do Ghibli and Frieren each have their own cadence? Is there a right and wrong timing, or is it whatever I want to express — and how would I translate a thought like nervous, or drowsy, or excited, into timing?”

Asked immediately after re-timing the blink, on noticing it suddenly “looked like a Ghibli film.” The observation that prompted it is the thesis of this whole track: the meaning of a shot changed without a single pixel being redrawn. Same five drawings, different distribution of time. Emotion lives in the timing, not in the drawings. (Norman McLaren: “Animation is not the art of drawings that move, but the art of movements that are drawn.”)

Two levels: the physics floor, and authorship above it

  • The physics floor — here there is genuine right and wrong. A heavy object that stops instantly is wrong. A falling thing that does not accelerate is wrong. Mass, gravity, inertia and follow-through are not stylistic opinions; violate them and the shot reads as amateur regardless of intent. This floor is what T3-02, the bouncing prop, exists to build.
  • Above the floor — everything is authorship. Once the physics reads, hold length, snap, and anticipation are voice.

The rule: physics is the grammar; timing is the prose style. One cannot write beautifully in broken grammar — but correct grammar alone is not a voice either.

The seven dials of timing

Timing is not one control. Naming the dials is what makes it usable deliberately rather than by feel:

  • Speed — frames per move
  • Cadence — ones / twos / threes
  • Hold length — how long stillness lasts
  • Easing — how bunched the spacing is at the start and end of a move
  • Anticipation — the counter-move before the action
  • Overshoot & settle — whether motion passes its target and returns
  • Regularity — whether the rhythm is even or irregular

Translating a feeling into timing

  • Nervous — small amplitude, fast, irregular rhythm, short jittery holds, little easing (abrupt starts and stops), frequent changes of direction. The key is unpredictability: nervousness is timing that cannot be anticipated.
  • Drowsy — long holds, slow moves, heavy easing at both ends, low amplitude, a downward drift with a small catch-and-recover. On threes or fours.
  • Excited — fast, large amplitude, short anticipation, big overshoots past the target, quick recovery, bursts on ones, almost no holds.
  • Sad / heavy — slow, long ease-in, no overshoot (the energy simply dies), very long holds, everything settling downward.
  • Confident — moderate speed, clear anticipation, a decisive snap, and perfectly still holds; stillness without jitter reads as control.

Worth noticing: nervous and excited share high speed, and are separated almost entirely by regularity and overshoot. That is the precision this vocabulary affords.

The signatures of Ghibli and Frieren

  • Ghibli — ma (間), the pause. Documented first-hand: at Toronto in 2002 Roger Ebert told Miyazaki he loved the “gratuitous motion” in his films — characters sitting, sighing, gazing at a running stream, doing something extra that does not advance the plot. Miyazaki replied: “We have a word for that in Japanese. It’s called ‘ma.’ Emptiness. It’s there intentionally.” He clapped three or four times: “The time in between my clapping is ‘ma.’ If you just have non-stop action with no breathing space at all, it’s just busyness.” And: “The people who make the movies are scared of silence.” Ghibli also spends real drawings on mundane physical effort — cooking, hauling, walking uphill — which is why their worlds feel weighted.
  • Frieren — stillness against burst. Long, near-static holds on faces, then sudden explosive fluidity in combat. The contrast is the signature. And it is thematically loaded: the series concerns an elf’s relationship to time, so the slowness is an argument, not merely a budget.

Style is timing plus design, colour, staging, and the choice of what to animate at all — but timing is the layer an audience feels and cannot name.
