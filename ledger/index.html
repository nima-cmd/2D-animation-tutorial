Front matter

On the four tracks, and why they are not in order

Question asked: “Track 4 is done and we’re on Track 3, but we never did Track 2 — are we going out of order, or am I reading these wrong?”

The numbers are subject labels, not a sequence. Only Track 3 is a spine; the others are threads pulled in at the moment they are needed.

  • Track 4 (pen & tablet) came first because it gates everything — no usable pen, no drawing of any kind. It is a parallel thread, not a phase.
  • Track 3 starts early on purpose. The classic exercises — bouncing ball, flour sack — are deliberately built from circles and blobs. The tradition begins there precisely so that motion can be taught with shapes that demand no drawing skill. Waiting until you can draw well before animating anything is backwards.
  • Track 2 (drawing) is deferred, not skipped. It unlocks when an animation exercise starts hurting without it: T3-03 the flour sack needs T2-02 form & volume (a sack must feel like it has mass), and T3-04 the walk cycle needs T2-03 construction (you cannot animate a body you cannot build). The bouncing prop needs neither — it is a circle.

The principle underneath: learn a skill at the moment you have a concrete use for it, rather than doing four lessons on faith. A lesson marked locked in the Progress ledger therefore means “not needed yet,” not “not earned yet.”

Chapter I

Track 1 — Tools & workflow

The universal machinery of digital 2D animation — the tools found in every serious program (Krita, OpenToonz, Toon Boom…), so that graduating to OpenToonz means relearning button locations, not ideas.

Onion skinning

The digital lightbox. It shows faint coloured “ghosts” of the neighbouring frames beneath the one you are drawing, so each new drawing can be lined up against the last — the single tool that makes smooth frame-by-frame motion possible.

  • Red ghost = the previous frame; green ghost = the next (set by the colour keys at the bottom of the Onion Skins docker). Standing on a later frame, the earlier drawing sits behind you in red.
  • Two switches must both be on or nothing shows: the Onion Skins docker’s main toggle, and the per-layer light-bulb in the Layers docker.
  • Gotcha we hit: that light-bulb is camouflaged — far-left edge of the layer row, to the left of the eyeball, and it only appears on hover. If the ghosts won’t show, this bulb is almost always why.
  • Choose which neighbours appear with the number strip (-2 -1 0 1 2) in the docker — the negative numbers are the previous frames.

Holds, and animating “on twos”

A drawing lasts until the next keyframe replaces it — the gap between keyframes is the exposure. Two distinct uses of that gap:

  • A hold is the same drawing kept on screen while nothing changes — deliberate stillness (a character breathing, a landscape settling).
  • “On twos” is the working cadence for movement: a new drawing every 2 frames, each in a new position. The subject is moving the whole time — you’re just making ~12 drawings a second instead of 24. Not the same as a hold.

Question asked: “If it’s moving, why hold every drawing for two frames — why twos at all?”

  • The eye can’t tell. ~12 image-changes/second already reads as smooth; doubling to 24 adds little for most motion.
  • Half the drawings. Hand-drawn means one picture at a time — twos is half the labour, which is why every studio (Disney, Ghibli, anime) standardised on it.
  • The look. On-twos crispness is part of the hand-drawn visual language; on-ones can feel uncannily “video-like.”
  • When to break it: fast motion → drop to ones (else it strobes); calm → longer holds. Twos is the baseline, not a rule. A Frieren scene mixes all three — dialogue on twos, a slash on ones, a quiet gaze on a big hold.
  • Spacing = speed: the distance a subject jumps between successive drawings is what sells how fast it’s going. Spacing so wide it strobes is your cue to switch to ones.

Reusing drawings — duplicate frames & layers-as-cels

Question asked: “Drawing the body by hand took ages — do I have to draw the whole thing over again on the next frame?” No. Two ways animation avoids redrawing what doesn’t change:

  • Create Duplicate Frame copies the entire current drawing into the new frame — you then erase/redraw only the part that changes (for a blink, just the eyes). The body comes along for free; you never redraw it.
  • Layers as cels (the pro method): put the parts that don’t move on their own layer (the fuzzy body) and hold it — one drawing, never redrawn — and animate only the moving part (the eyes) on a layer above. This is exactly how hand-drawn studios worked: stacked transparent cels over a single held background painting. For slow, laborious drawing this is the biggest time-saver there is — draw the hard part once.

Re-timing: moving keyframes without redrawing anything

Timing is editable after the fact. A drawing is not welded to its frame — drag a keyframe along the timeline and the drawing travels with it. An entire re-time costs zero new drawings, which makes timing the cheapest thing in animation to experiment with, and therefore the thing to experiment with most.

  • Work in the direction you are moving. Shifting keyframes earlier (0,3,6,9,12 → 0,2,3,5,8), go left to right: each move vacates the slot the next one needs, so nothing ever lands on an occupied frame. Shifting later, go right to left. Getting this backwards makes one keyframe drop onto another and destroy it.
  • Save a copy before re-timing (-retimed suffix). Keeping the original beside the new version is what lets you play the two back to back — and the comparison is where the learning actually is.
  • If you save over the original anyway (done here, 2026-08-11): Krita keeps a backup beside the file named <name>.kra~, which holds the document as it was before the most recent save. The original was recovered from it intact. But the window is exactly one save wide — the next save rewrites .kra~ with the new version and the original is gone for good. On discovering the mistake, copy the backup somewhere safe before touching Krita again, and immediately File → Save As onto the intended name so the open document stops pointing at the file you meant to protect.
  • A .kra is a zip. The exact keyframe times sit in <name>/layers/<layer>.keyframes.xml. For a pure re-time — where no drawing changed — those times are the whole edit, so the timing can be verified to the frame without rendering anything.

Building a rig — separating independent variables onto layers

With everything on one paint layer, every keyframe is a copy of the whole drawing — which is why the first soot sprite needed six near-identical copies of a laborious fuzzy body. Splitting the parts that move independently onto their own layers changes the arithmetic:

3 lid states × 3 gaze states = 9 combinations, from 6 drawings rather than 9. Add a fourth lid state and you get 12 combinations for the price of one drawing. Independent variables on separate layers cost addition; on one layer they cost multiplication.

This is also the real answer to “six drawings, one axis” — every drawing of the first sprite moved the lid vertically, so a horizontal idea like gaze was impossible. Layers are how a second axis gets bought cheaply. When planning, count your axes, not your drawings.

The layer order that works (and the obvious one is wrong) — bottom to top:

  • body / fuzz — static, never redrawn. Keep the eye area solid; the whites paint over it.
  • eye white — static, never redrawn.
  • pupil — animates the gaze.
  • lid — animates the blink, and must be an opaque shape that covers.

The gotcha: if the pupil sits above the lid, a closed eye shows two black dots floating on the lid. A real eyelid is opaque and covers — so the lid goes on top, and the eye white becomes a single static drawing while the lid does all the blinking.

Draw the neutral pose more open than you need. A rest pose you can move away from in both directions gives room to close and to widen later for a startle; one drawn already half-lidded can only close.

Rig file as a drawing library. Park each drawing on its own early frame — pupils centre / left / right at frames 0, 1, 2 — and keep the rig separate from the shots that use it (Save As per shot). The rig stays pristine, a botched shot never costs the character, and it approximates how production separates a model from its scenes. It is also the nearest thing Krita offers to OpenToonz's numbered drawings in a level.

Keyframe colour labels — and a save trap

Krita cannot name a keyframe. Layers have names; individual drawings do not. The only per-keyframe marker is a colour label, so the legend has to live outside the file — the way an exposure sheet's legend sits at the top of the page rather than on each drawing.

Why bother: Krita renumbers the underlying frame files on save. A drawing that was layer2.f4 becomes layer2.f8 after an unrelated edit, so filenames are not stable identity. Colour labels are the only thing that survives, and they travel with a drawing when it is copied into a shot — where position no longer identifies it.

The trap (hit twice, 2026-08-19): colour-labelling a keyframe does not mark the document as modified. Krita therefore has nothing to save, Ctrl+S silently does nothing, and the labels are gone on reopen. Workaround: make a real change as well, or close and reopen to verify the labels actually persisted.

Colour → stored integer, confirmed by inspecting saved files: 1 = blue, 2 = green, 3 = yellow. Others not yet identified.

The exposure sheet — think in durations, not frame numbers

The traditional planning document (also called an X-sheet or dope sheet): two columns — the frame number, and which drawing sits on it. Krita’s timeline is the same information turned on its side.

Question asked: “What do you mean, derive the sheet?” The answer corrects a natural misreading: frame numbers are not a decision — they are a running total. You choose how long each drawing stays on screen; the frame column falls out by addition.

  • A drawing lasting 4 frames starting at 0 → the next begins at 4.
  • That one lasting 2 → the next begins at 6.
  • Each frame number is the previous number plus the previous duration.

This is precisely why a physical exposure sheet is ruled as a long column of frames: you read down it and the arithmetic is already done. The creative work is entirely in the duration column; converting to frame numbers is bookkeeping.

Why bother when the timeline shows it anyway: a list of frame numbers alone is ambiguous, and the ambiguity hides dropped in-betweens. Writing the drawing name beside each frame is what exposed a missing half in the drowsy blink — the eye would have cut straight from open to shut. Name the drawings and the gap announces itself.

Question asked: “Is the exposure sheet still used? What does one actually look like?” Checked 2026-08-14 — sources in references.md.

  • It is vertical. Time runs down the page, not across. Krita’s timeline is the same information rotated ninety degrees.
  • Rows are frames — one row, one frame. Western sheets run 80–96 rows to a page; Japanese time sheets (タイムシート) run 144 rows = exactly 6 seconds at 24fps, with a heavy rule every 24 rows marking off each second so duration reads at a glance.
  • Columns are layers, lettered A, B, C… with A nearest the background and later letters nearer the camera — the cel stack of Chapter I, formalised. Further columns carry action notes, dialogue broken down phonetically, background numbers, and camera instructions.
  • Notation: a circled number is a key drawing (genga); a dash is an in-between (douga); and a vertical line ruled straight down the column is a hold.

That last convention is the whole of “a gap is a hold, not a movement”, solved on paper. Traditional animators drew a literal line through every frame where the drawing does not change, making it visually impossible to mistake a hold for motion. The drowsy blink’s 6/8/10 error would have been unmissable on a sheet: three lines getting longer, and a line means nothing is happening. The digital timeline, by hiding this behind empty space, makes the mistake easy to commit.

Still current, and specifically where this course is heading: digital X-sheets exist in Toon Boom Harmony and Adobe Animate, and OpenToonz’s Xsheet is one of its main rooms, not a legacy view — so this notation is the actual interface waiting at the end of Track 1 (T1-03).

In-betweens (met early, in the blink)

Between two key drawings you can add in-between drawings that ease from one to the next — a blink reads far smoother as open → half → shut → half → open than as a hard open/shut cut. How those in-betweens are spaced in time (even vs. uneven, fast vs. slow) is what makes motion feel alive or mechanical — first met here in T1-02, drilled properly in Track 3’s principles lessons.

Chapter III

Track 3 — Animation fundamentals

The classic milestone sequence from the Disney tradition — Thomas & Johnston’s twelve principles, Williams’ Survival Kit — worked here as ball, sack, walk, scene.

Frame rate is the grid; cadence is what you do on it

Question asked: “Was I supposed to do this at 12fps? I don’t understand where fps determines whether I draw in twos or threes, or how I’d choose my fps.”

These are two different numbers, and untangling them is most of what timing is:

  • Frame rate is the grid — how finely the second is divided. At 24fps there are 24 slots per second, each ≈42ms. It is a property of the document, set once.
  • Ones / twos / threes is the cadence — how many of those slots each drawing occupies. It is not a setting anywhere. It is purely where you place your keyframes. That is the entire mechanism.
  • 24fps, a drawing every 1 slot → 24 drawings/sec — on ones
  • 24fps, a drawing every 2 slots → 12 drawings/sec — on twos (the standard)
  • 24fps, a drawing every 3 slots → 8 drawings/sec — on threes

So “12fps” as a working rate means 12 drawings per second, achieved by spacing keyframes 2 apart on a 24fps timeline — not by setting the document to 12. Setting the document to 12 would make 12 drawings/sec a ceiling: a fast action needing ones becomes impossible. Keeping the fine grid lets you choose cadence per shot — dialogue on twos, a slash on ones, a held gaze on a long hold.

The rule: fps is the grid, cadence is what you do on it. Set the grid once (24 — film and anime standard), then vary cadence constantly. Choose 30 or 60 only for video and game work, never for traditional 2D.

Where to read it in Krita: Animation Timeline docker, bottom bar, right-hand end — a small box reading 24 (tooltip Frame rate). The percentage box beside it is play speed, a preview-only slow-motion that changes nothing in the file — do not confuse them. Definitive fallback: a .kra is a zip; inside maindoc.xml sits <framerate value="24">.

Changing cadence within a single action

Cadence is not one choice per shot — it can shift inside one movement, and that shift is what makes motion feel alive. The re-timed blink:

  • open @0 → half @2 — twos
  • half @2 → shut @3 — ones (the lids slamming shut: the fastest instant)
  • shut @3 → half @5 — twos
  • half @5 → open @8 — threes (the eye lazily reopening)

Contrast the first attempt, which used threes for all four gaps. The eye travelled at the same rate for the whole blink — and real things do not. Uniform cadence is the signature of mechanical motion. Fast in, slow out, is the signature of a living one.

Note: those numbers were later corrected against measured physiology to 0 / 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 — see Deriving frame counts from measurement below. The principle is unchanged; the arithmetic was off.

Deriving frame counts from measurement — the five steps

Question asked: “Why those keyframes? I don’t want to be told how to make something look right — I want the toolset to control it myself.” The correct answer to “why these numbers” is never taste; it is an arithmetic chain that can be re-run on any action:

  1. What does the real thing take, in milliseconds? Measure it, film yourself, or find a study.
  2. Divide by the frame duration — at 24fps one frame is 41.67 ms — to get a frame count.
  3. Find the asymmetry. Nearly every action has a fast phase and a slow phase. Split the frame budget by the measured ratio, not by halves.
  4. Within each phase, is it accelerating or decelerating? Accelerating → the time gaps shrink. Decelerating → the gaps grow.
  5. Then stylise. Usually compress for snap. This is the only step where taste enters — steps 1–4 can be objectively wrong; step 5 cannot.

The blink, derived properly (and an instructor error corrected)

Claude first asserted from memory that a blink closes twice as fast as it reopens (1:2). Checked against measurement, that was wrong. VanderWerf et al. (2003), using a magnetic search coil on the lid with EMG of the orbicularis oculi, measured the spontaneous blink as:

  • total 334 ± 67 ms
  • closing (down) phase 92 ± 17 ms
  • opening (up) phase 242 ± 55 ms — nearly three times the close, a ratio of about 1 : 2.6

Re-running the five steps at 24fps: total 334 ÷ 41.67 = 8 frames; close 92 ÷ 41.67 = 2 frames; open 242 ÷ 41.67 = 6 frames. Distributing the reopen with widening gaps (2, then 4) gives:

open @0 · half @1 · shut @2 · half @4 · open @8 — 333 ms against a measured 334 ms.

The first attempt (0/2/3/5/8) closed one frame too slowly and reopened one frame too briefly. The lesson is not the numbers but the method: a claim from memory produced plausible keyframes that were quietly wrong, and only the measurement exposed it.

A limit worth knowing: at 24fps the entire closing phase is 2 frames — the grid barely resolves it, leaving no room to show the lids accelerating. This is precisely why animators frequently cut straight from open to shut with no in-between: the physiology was never available at this resolution to begin with.

speed = spacing ÷ timing — the two levers

Observed by the user, unprompted, on comparing the three versions of the blink: “keyframes closer together for faster animation — the first three keyframes, the eyes closing, are back to back, and that’s where we want our speed. Inversely, when the eyes open we want to go slower, and the keyframes are spaced farther apart.” Correct — and worth stating in its general form, because the blink is the special case:

  • Spacing — how far the subject moved in position between two drawings.
  • Timing — how many frames sit between them.
  • Speed = spacing ÷ timing. Distance over time; literally velocity. Which means there are two independent levers on speed, and most exercises only hand you one.

Why the blink only offered one: its five drawings are fixed, so the positional distance between “open” and “half-shut” is baked into the artwork and cannot change without redrawing. Spacing was frozen; time was the only variable. Compressing the frames covers the same distance in less time — faster.

The bouncing prop (T3-02) inverts this exactly: timing is held constant (on twos throughout) and the ball is drawn in a different place each time — gaps wide while it falls fast, bunching tight as it slows at the top of the arc. Same drawings per second, and it still reads as accelerating and decelerating. That is spacing working alone, with timing frozen.

Blink = the time lever. Ball = the position lever. Animation is driving both at once.

Note also the shape of the reopening: gaps of 2 then 4 — doubling. Widening gaps are a deceleration curve, and drawing them out as a diagram is precisely Williams’ spacing chart.

Inverting the asymmetry — where an emotion actually lives

Found by the user while deriving a drowsy blink from the same five drawings, reasoning about what a tired eyelid is doing rather than reaching for “the same thing, slower”: “you start fighting to keep your eyes open, so the lids drop slowly; then you lose the battle and it accelerates shut; you hold there as you doze; then the last two frames are in succession to mirror the frantic, jolting reopening as you catch yourself.”

  • A waking blink: fast close (92 ms), slow open (242 ms) — ratio ≈ 1 : 2.6.
  • A drowsy blink: slow close (208 ms), fast open (83 ms) — ratio ≈ 2.5 : 1.

The emotion is not in the duration; it is in the reversal. An alert eye snaps shut and drifts open. An eye fighting sleep sags shut and snaps open. Stretching every gap uniformly would only have produced a slow blink — the characterisation comes from flipping which phase is the fast one. Look for the asymmetry first, and ask whether the feeling inverts it.

And note what carried the meaning: a hold. The most expressive moment in the shot is the one where nothing moves. That is ma — Miyazaki’s pause — arrived at from the opposite direction, and a reminder that stillness is an active choice rather than an absence of animation. Corollary: a jolt reads as frantic only in proportion to the stillness it interrupts, so lengthening the hold buys a sharper recovery for free.

A gap is a hold, not a movement — where the time lever runs out

The single hardest thing to internalise about a timeline: the frames between two keyframes do not show motion. They show the earlier drawing, held still. Motion is an illusion assembled from the cuts between drawings, not from the gaps.

How this bites (found in the drowsy blink, 2026-08-14): with only open, half and shut available, the intent “each droop is slower and heavier than the last” was expressed by lengthening the gaps between open and half — 6, 8, then 10 frames. But with no drawing between those two positions, the eye does not descend; it cuts. So the gap does not render as “a slow droop.” It renders as “the eye sat fully open for 10 frames.”

The result was the exact opposite of the intent: lengthening open-exposures reads as a man holding his eyes open longer each round — winning the fight, not losing it.

Two remedies, priced differently:

  • Re-time within the drawings you have (free). Shorten the open exposures — 6, 4, 2 — so each recovery buys less waking time than the last. That is the losing arc, and it is expressible without new artwork.
  • Draw the in-between (the real answer). Add a three-quarters open lid so the descent has a middle: open → ¾ → half. Only then can a long gap genuinely read as slow sinking.

The general law: timing can only redistribute the drawings you have; it cannot invent positions between them. When an intent needs a quality of movement rather than a duration, the time lever has run out and the answer is a new drawing. Recognising that boundary is what stops re-timing from becoming a substitute for animating.

Straight ahead vs pose to pose — and why the middle went robotic

Question asked: “Is building the start and figuring out the ending from it the way to go, or should I start with the ending and work backwards?” — asked after blocking the whole nervous shot in one sitting and finding that the first half worked and the second lost all life.

This is Straight Ahead Action and Pose to Pose, one of the twelve principles:

  • Straight ahead — animate forward, discovering as you go. Fluid and spontaneous; its documented weakness is that it drifts and loses structure the further it runs from the start.
  • Pose to pose — establish the key poses first, then fill the gaps. Controlled, holds its shape.

He animated straight ahead and met both halves of it exactly: “in some instances I'm finding the character as I'm animating” (the genuine strength) and “after that I lose it and the character becomes robotic” (the known cost).

The answer is neither start-first nor end-first — it is both ends before the middle. He had discovered half of this already: “knowing I was gonna end at 200 helped me build within the constraint and find the timing better.” But that was a fixed frame number with no pose to arrive at. A destination makes the span solvable, because every choice is then measured against where it must land rather than trailing away from where it began.

Diagnostic worth keeping: when a section marks time, check whether it is heading anywhere. A middle often goes slack not because its timing is wrong but because the ending it should be approaching does not exist yet.

Gaze: drift in, dart out — and why you cannot drift across centre

Real gaze has two physiologically distinct movement types, and they cost different things to animate:

  • Saccade — fast, jerk-like, 20–200ms. At 24fps that is sub-frame, so it is a cut: no in-betweens exist, and drawing them would be wrong.
  • Drift — slow, smooth, irregular and involuntary, occurring during attempted fixation. This one needs in-betweens.

And the fact that makes them a story: holding an off-centre gaze produces slow centripetal drift — the eye slides back toward straight-ahead and needs a corrective saccade to stay put. So “the more you try not to look at it, the more you are drawn to it” is not merely psychological; the eye is mechanically pulled toward the thing being avoided.

Three rules follow, and between them they type every transition automatically — no need to mark drift or dart by hand:

  • Toward centre, same side → DRIFT. Involuntary; the eye slides.
  • Away from centre, same side → DART. Deliberate effort; a cut.
  • Crossing centre → DART, necessarily. A drift would pass through centre — and passing through centre means making eye contact. This is why nervous eyes flick rather than sweep: the flick exists to skip the thing being avoided.

Consequence for design: a character can circle near centre indefinitely using crossing darts, and only falls in when a move toward centre is allowed to drift. Capture is a change of movement type, not of position.

Compose in phrases, not positions

Found by the user after listing his options — left→right, left→centre, left→slight-left→centre, left→slight-left→left→centre — which is not a list of transitions but a grammar. Each is a small complete event with its own shape. The best of them, left ~ slight-left → left ~ centre, reads as resistance failing: he drifts toward the camera, catches himself and pulls back, then goes anyway.

Why the altitude matters: planning twenty-one individual positions is unmanageable, which is why a long route feels arbitrary while you write it. Four or five phrases, each a complete approach-and-escape, is a shot that can actually be reasoned about — the same reason music is composed in bars rather than note by note.

And phrases take an arc, exactly as frames do. If every phrase arrives at centre it is one failure repeated — the metronome problem one level up. If the arrivals escalate (near-miss, longer near-miss, arrival, arrival that lingers) you get a man losing a fight. The decaying catches of the drowsy blink, applied to phrases instead of frames.

Structure can be spatial, too. The nervous shot's five phrases contract: P1 uses only the extremes, and by P4 the eye is confined to the near-centre positions before falling in — a decaying orbit. Tracking distance-from-centre per phrase revealed a shape the position list alone did not show. Plot the amplitude of a route and see whether it means anything.

Animate the channel that carries the story first

The user's own working rule, stated while planning the nervous shot: “the pupil animation should happen first — the pupils tell the story, the lids then contribute. It's easier to adjust the lid to match the story the pupil is telling than the other way around.”

This is secondary action, one of Thomas & Johnston's twelve: a supporting action enriches the main one without competing with it. The workflow that follows — block the primary first, layer the secondary onto it — is standard practice.

But the sharper claim is a hierarchy, not a convenience. Timing the blinks first would mean bending the gaze to fit a lid rhythm — letting the decoration dictate the meaning. Deciding which channel is primary is therefore a story judgement made before any frames exist, and it settles the order of work. Ask of any shot: which channel would still carry the meaning if every other one were removed? That one goes first.

Worth noting that the rig created this choice. On a single layer both parts must be drawn together, every frame a combination — there is no ordering decision to make. Separating them is what turned “which first?” into a question that can be answered well or badly.

A second animated layer is a moving hold

Found by the user while planning the nervous blink: “we don't want the animation to ever feel dead, so if we hold on a left too long we risk that — but it also adds the ability to add blinking, and another whole layer of showing nervousness.”

With every part on one layer, a long fixation is a frozen drawing. Split gaze and lid onto separate layers and the lid can move during a gaze hold — so the look-away is never dead. The second layer is the moving hold, and it comes free. That is the payoff of the rig beyond its drawing-count arithmetic: independent channels mean one can carry stillness while the other carries life.

The general form: when a hold goes dead, the fix need not be a new drawing on the same part — it can be movement on a different one. Breath, hair, a blink, a background element. This is why studios animate secondary elements at all.

Blinks are part of gaze shifts, not separate from them

Checked 2026-08-20, because a design decision rested on it. The user's instinct was to blink before changing gaze direction. The physiology says something slightly different:

  • Many vertebrates generate blinks as a component of saccadic gaze shifts — blinks are integrated into the motor command for a gaze shift rather than being independent reflexes.
  • Lid-closing muscle activity accompanies 97% of gaze shifts larger than 33°; in humans, large shifts raise blink likelihood to roughly 20%.
  • Blink probability increases with gaze amplitude — bigger darts want blinks, small ones often do not.
  • The blink begins simultaneously with the eye movement, not before it. Saccades accompanying blinks are also measurably slower.

Design rules that follow: put the blink on the dart rather than ahead of it, and reserve blinks for the larger direction changes rather than every one.

Claude's inference, not sourced: since a saccade is sub-frame and therefore a cut, a blink landing on the same frame would mask that cut — the lid covers the eye exactly as the pupil jumps. Real vision works this way, and it suggests blinks could be used deliberately to hide the pop on the largest darts. Untested; worth trying against a version without it.

The moving hold — why a long freeze reads as dead

Diagnosed by the user, unprompted, from watching his own drowsy blink: “once we hit 24 all tension kind of stops, cause we just hold on the one image.” This is a named principle. Thomas & Johnston developed the moving hold, and the canonical statement is almost the same sentence: freezing a character completely the moment the main pose is reached reads as dead. The remedy is not a shorter hold but a pose held without being static — small controlled movement rather than a total freeze. Even a character doing nothing breathes and settles.

A moving hold cannot be faked with timing. No arrangement of existing drawings makes a frozen stretch anything other than frozen; it requires another drawing. This was the third time in one exercise the time lever ran out, and the first time the answer was unavoidably new artwork — see A gap is a hold, not a movement.

What the missing drawing turned out to be: the shot had two states collapsed into one. A single shut was doing the work of both “just closed — still tense, still fighting” and “gone — everything released.” Drawing the second turned twenty dead frames into the moment he actually goes under: the tension no longer stops, it releases, visibly. When a hold feels dead, ask what two states it is being asked to cover.

Stillness reads as dead only when the pose implies effort

Follow-on, and the more useful half. With one switch, a 20-frame hold splits into two static stretches — either way one is long. Moving the switch does not remove the stillness; it relocates it. Placing the change at frame 36 left 12 frames of the tense drawing; at frame 31, 13 frames of the released one. The second reads far better, and the difference is not duration:

  • A clamped, tense eye motionless for 500ms contradicts itself — the pose says something is happening, the timing says nothing is.
  • A released eye motionless for 542ms is doing exactly what a sleeping face does. The pose earns the stillness.

So a pose implying effort has a short shelf life; a pose implying rest can hold almost indefinitely. When a held drawing goes dead, the question is not only “how long” but “does this pose deserve to be still?” The user's own summary: “the longer hold after the new frame leaves the deadness less visible, since it fits the narrative more than having it before.”

Does the state oscillate, or does it change?

The first instinct for reviving a dead hold was to alternate between the two shut drawings — A-B-A-B — for more life. A real technique, and the wrong shape here.

  • Alternation means ongoing activity — something repeating, therefore something still happening. It is how breathing, hovering, shivering and idling are animated.
  • Going under is a one-way release. It changes state and stays there.
  • A-B-A-B is also perfectly uniform repetition — the very thing that made the first blink read mechanical. It would have replaced dead air with a buzz.

Match the shape to the thing: breathing oscillates, collapse changes. Ask which before reaching for a cycle.

The first exposure sets the baseline — and can imply off-screen history

Found by the user on comparing two drowsy blinks differing only in their opening exposure — 4 / 8 / 2 against 8 / 8 / 2: “the length was too short and the next one is long, so it does seem like you're walking in the middle of him dozing off, as opposed to from the start.”

Claude's formalisation of it: the opening exposure is the reference against which every later one is judged. Consequences:

  • Beat 1 shorter than beat 2 → the viewer infers earlier, longer beats they did not see, and reads the shot as joined late, already in progress.
  • Beat 1 longest, or equal to what follows → the shot reads as beginning.

So implied off-screen history is available for free, purely from where the first number sits relative to the second — no extra drawings, no extra frames. This is the deliberate way to open a shot mid-action.

The related distinction between the two versions, which is not visible in “167ms versus 333ms”: compare the first open exposure with the one after the first catch. 4 → 8 means the catch won — he recovers to better than he started, so the eventual collapse is a surprise. 8 → 8 means the catch merely restored him to baseline — nothing improves, it holds and then fails. One is a fight with a victory in it; the other is a wall that does not crack until it does. Neither is more correct; that is the actual choice.

Timing is read relatively, not absolutely

Found by the user on lengthening the drowsy blink’s hold: “when I increased the length of the shut, I needed to further slow down the shutting of the eyes at the beginning, and add one extra hold before the eyes open, to keep things in scale and balance.”

The close had not been touched — yet it now felt too fast. A duration has no speed of its own; it is perceived against its neighbours. 208 ms beside a 208 ms hold reads as measured; the identical 208 ms beside an 875 ms hold reads as hurried.

Consequence: stretching one element silently changes the read of every element around it. Timing must be rebalanced as a whole, never adjusted one gap at a time. This is why a shot is judged on playback rather than in the numbers — and it is the same reason a jolt reads as frantic only in proportion to the stillness it interrupts.

Re-using drawings out of order (non-monotonic keyframes)

A keyframe need not introduce a new drawing — it can re-place one already made. A sequence may therefore double back: open → half → open → half → shut.

Why it matters: a lid that only ever descends is a surrender; a lid that briefly climbs back is a struggle. Any action containing conflict, hesitation, tremor, or repetition needs this — and it costs no new artwork, only copied keyframes. It is the technique on which a nervous read almost entirely depends, since nervousness is irregularity and irregularity requires revisiting positions.

Timing as expression — is there a right and a wrong?

Question asked: “Is timing what gives a film its signature? Do Ghibli and Frieren each have their own cadence? Is there a right and wrong timing, or is it whatever I want to express — and how would I translate a thought like nervous, or drowsy, or excited, into timing?”

Asked immediately after re-timing the blink, on noticing it suddenly “looked like a Ghibli film.” The observation that prompted it is the thesis of this whole track: the meaning of a shot changed without a single pixel being redrawn. Same five drawings, different distribution of time. Emotion lives in the timing, not in the drawings. (Norman McLaren: “Animation is not the art of drawings that move, but the art of movements that are drawn.”)

Two levels: the physics floor, and authorship above it

  • The physics floor — here there is genuine right and wrong. A heavy object that stops instantly is wrong. A falling thing that does not accelerate is wrong. Mass, gravity, inertia and follow-through are not stylistic opinions; violate them and the shot reads as amateur regardless of intent. This floor is what T3-02, the bouncing prop, exists to build.
  • Above the floor — everything is authorship. Once the physics reads, hold length, snap, and anticipation are voice.

The rule: physics is the grammar; timing is the prose style. One cannot write beautifully in broken grammar — but correct grammar alone is not a voice either.

The seven dials of timing

Timing is not one control. Naming the dials is what makes it usable deliberately rather than by feel:

  • Speed — frames per move
  • Cadence — ones / twos / threes
  • Hold length — how long stillness lasts
  • Easing — how bunched the spacing is at the start and end of a move
  • Anticipation — the counter-move before the action
  • Overshoot & settle — whether motion passes its target and returns
  • Regularity — whether the rhythm is even or irregular

Translating a feeling into timing

  • Nervous — small amplitude, fast, irregular rhythm, short jittery holds, little easing (abrupt starts and stops), frequent changes of direction. The key is unpredictability: nervousness is timing that cannot be anticipated.
  • Drowsy — long holds, slow moves, heavy easing at both ends, low amplitude, a downward drift with a small catch-and-recover. On threes or fours.
  • Excited — fast, large amplitude, short anticipation, big overshoots past the target, quick recovery, bursts on ones, almost no holds.
  • Sad / heavy — slow, long ease-in, no overshoot (the energy simply dies), very long holds, everything settling downward.
  • Confident — moderate speed, clear anticipation, a decisive snap, and perfectly still holds; stillness without jitter reads as control.

Worth noticing: nervous and excited share high speed, and are separated almost entirely by regularity and overshoot. That is the precision this vocabulary affords.

The signatures of Ghibli and Frieren

  • Ghibli — ma (間), the pause. Documented first-hand: at Toronto in 2002 Roger Ebert told Miyazaki he loved the “gratuitous motion” in his films — characters sitting, sighing, gazing at a running stream, doing something extra that does not advance the plot. Miyazaki replied: “We have a word for that in Japanese. It’s called ‘ma.’ Emptiness. It’s there intentionally.” He clapped three or four times: “The time in between my clapping is ‘ma.’ If you just have non-stop action with no breathing space at all, it’s just busyness.” And: “The people who make the movies are scared of silence.” Ghibli also spends real drawings on mundane physical effort — cooking, hauling, walking uphill — which is why their worlds feel weighted.
  • Frieren — stillness against burst. Long, near-static holds on faces, then sudden explosive fluidity in combat. The contrast is the signature. And it is thematically loaded: the series concerns an elf’s relationship to time, so the slowness is an argument, not merely a budget.

Style is timing plus design, colour, staging, and the choice of what to animate at all — but timing is the layer an audience feels and cannot name.
